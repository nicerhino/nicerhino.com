Over the past century, the United States has played a dominant role in shaping the global order. From the post-World War II reconstruction to the establishment of institutions like the United Nations, the World Bank, and NATO, U.S. leadership has been central to maintaining a relatively stable, liberal international system ([Ikenberry, 2011](https://www.jstor.org/stable/23039408)). But in recent years, a clear retrenchment has emerged. Whether due to domestic political polarisation, strategic fatigue, or shifting economic priorities, the United States is increasingly retreating from its traditional role as the world’s anchor of liberal values and global governance.

This trend accelerated dramatically during the Trump administration (2017–2021), which embraced an "America First" doctrine that explicitly rejected many of the pillars of U.S.-led internationalism. President Trump withdrew the U.S. from the Paris Climate Agreement, the Iran nuclear deal (JCPOA), and the Trans-Pacific Partnership (TPP), and severely weakened the World Trade Organisation by blocking appointments to its appellate body ([Council on Foreign Relations, 2021](https://www.cfr.org/report/reset-world-trade-organizations-appellate-body)). Rhetorically and diplomatically, the administration frequently undermined alliances such as NATO and questioned the value of long-standing multilateral commitments. These moves signalled a sharp departure from decades of bipartisan support for liberal international order.

While the Biden administration has sought to re-engage with allies and global institutions, the foundations of American global leadership have been shaken. The country remains deeply polarised, with isolationist and nationalist currents continuing to shape political discourse. According to the Pew Research Center, global confidence in the U.S. presidency dropped to historic lows during the Trump years ([Pew Research Center, 2020](https://www.pewresearch.org/global/2020/01/08/trump-ratings-remain-low-around-globe-while-views-of-u-s-stay-mostly-favorable/)), and while it has rebounded under Biden, skepticism remains about the reliability and continuity of U.S. commitments.

This withdrawal presents a moment of realignment—and opportunity. As American influence recedes, other nations and coalitions are stepping forward to fill the void. For countries and organisations committed to multilateralism, social responsibility, and free trade, this could be a defining moment.

## The Nature of the Withdrawal

The signs are increasingly difficult to ignore. The U.S. withdrawal from the Paris Climate Agreement (and subsequent reentry) signalled inconsistency on global cooperation. The chaotic exit from Afghanistan in 2021, reduced engagement in the World Trade Organisation, and diminished diplomatic presence in key multilateral forums underscore a broader trend: a pivot away from the burdens of global stewardship ([White House press release, 2025](https://www.whitehouse.gov/presidential-actions/2025/02/withdrawing-the-united-states-from-and-ending-funding-to-certain-united-nations-organizations-and-reviewing-united-states-support-to-all-international-organizations/)).

According to the Lowy Institute’s Global Diplomacy Index, between 2017 and 2021, the U.S. closed or downgraded numerous diplomatic posts, while China, by contrast, expanded its diplomatic network to become the world’s most diplomatically represented country ([Lowy Institute, 2021]()). This shift is emblematic of the broader repositioning underway.

## The Case for Regional Leadership

As the U.S. steps back, it creates room for regional powers and supranational organisations to assume greater responsibility. Nowhere is this more promising than in the European Union.

The EU, with its 27 member states and a population exceeding 447 million, represents the largest single market in the world ([World Bank, 2023]()). It embodies a commitment to democratic governance, human rights, environmental protection, and economic solidarity. Crucially, its model of shared sovereignty and economic interdependence is precisely what the world needs more of in an era of fragmentation.

The EU has already shown its capacity to lead. Its Green Deal is the most ambitious climate strategy in the world, aiming for net-zero emissions by 2050 ([European Commission, 2023]()). Through initiatives like the NextGenerationEU recovery plan, the EU is demonstrating how public investment can drive inclusive economic renewal ([European Commission, 2022]()). In trade, the EU has been a staunch advocate of multilateralism, finalising trade deals with Japan, Mercosur, and several African nations despite rising protectionism elsewhere.

## Historical Lessons and Future Prospects

History suggests that multipolarity can foster greater stability and innovation when underpinned by cooperative frameworks. The Concert of Europe in the 19th century, despite its flaws, helped maintain relative peace among great powers for decades ([Schroeder, 1994]()). More recently, the postwar Bretton Woods institutions—though U.S.-led—succeeded because they distributed power and benefits more broadly across allies.

We now have an opportunity to evolve beyond even that model. The future need not be one in which a single nation dominates, but rather one where networks of nations share leadership. EU expansion—both within Europe and through deeper ties with neighbouring regions—can serve as a blueprint. Likewise, the formation of EU-like blocs in other parts of the world, such as the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), could strengthen regional autonomy and reduce dependency on distant superpowers ([World Bank, 2020]()).

## The Moral Imperative

Beyond economics and strategy lies a moral obligation. In an interconnected world facing climate change, pandemics, migration crises, and digital disruption, we cannot afford disengagement. Wealthier nations and organisations have a responsibility to support their less fortunate neighbours not out of charity, but out of recognition of shared fate. The EU's cohesion funds, which transfer resources to poorer member states to level the playing field, exemplify this ethos ([European Parliament, 2022]()).

We must champion a world where cooperation, not coercion, drives progress. The U.S. stepping back from leadership is not necessarily a crisis. If embraced correctly, it is a chance to build a more pluralistic, just, and sustainable global order.

## Conclusion

The old model of American primacy is fading rapidly. What replaces it is still being shaped. For those who believe in free trade, social responsibility, and cooperative governance, this is not a time for nostalgia, but for action. Let us seize this opportunity to expand the circles of solidarity and elevate new voices in global leadership. In doing so, we may create a world that is not only more balanced, but more humane.
