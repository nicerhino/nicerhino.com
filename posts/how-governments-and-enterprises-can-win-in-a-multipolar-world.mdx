As US leadership on the global stage continues to recede, governments and enterprises face a pivotal moment. The rise of multipolar regional blocs presents not just geopolitical complexity, but new zones of opportunity. For those ready to think ahead, this is the time to invest, build partnerships, and shape the standards of the next global order.

1. For Governments: Influence Through Integration

Invest in Regional Frameworks: Governments can drive regional economic resilience by strengthening trade blocs. For example, the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) could increase African exports by $560 billion if fully implemented by 2035 (World Bank, 2020). Strategic infrastructure and regulatory harmonisation should be top priorities. According to the International Trade Centre, SMEs could see a 32% boost in exports if tariff and non-tariff barriers are removed (ITC, 2021).

Standard Setting as Soft Power: EU GDPR, once seen as niche, is now the global benchmark for data privacy. Over 120 countries have adopted or adapted data protection laws influenced by GDPR principles (UNCTAD, 2022). Governments should lead or influence the creation of global standards—for AI ethics, ESG reporting, or cybersecurity—to shape future trade dynamics and ensure domestic relevance.

Bilateral Stability Within Multilateralism: Governments should deepen bilateral ties inside multilateral structures. The Philippines-Japan infrastructure partnership, backed by over $12 billion in Japanese development assistance, is a case study in how targeted bilateral investment can reinforce regional coherence and strategic influence (JICA, 2023).

2. For Enterprises: Regionalism = Strategic Advantage

Localisation with Scale: Companies that succeed in a multipolar world will adapt products, partnerships, and policies to regional needs. Nestlé operates 300 factories in developing countries not for cost alone, but to meet local tastes, create resilience, and hedge geopolitical risk (Nestlé Annual Report, 2022).

Supply Chain Rewiring: The COVID-19 pandemic exposed the fragility of over-extended supply chains. Enterprises now have a chance to build regional hubs that are more self-sufficient and protected from transcontinental disruptions. A recent McKinsey report suggests that 45% of global trade flows could be economically reshored or nearshored by 2030 (McKinsey Global Institute, 2021).

Regulatory Foresight = First-Mover Advantage: Businesses that anticipate and align with emerging regional standards can enter markets faster and avoid compliance costs. The EU’s Digital Markets Act is reshaping how tech giants operate across borders. Similar initiatives are underway in India (the Digital Personal Data Protection Act) and Brazil (LGPD), pointing to a global trend of regulatory convergence (OECD, 2023).

3. Cross-Sector Collaboration: Building the Next Institutions

In a post-unipolar world, public-private collaboration matters more than ever. Think of the COVAX vaccine initiative, which distributed over 2 billion doses globally by 2022, or green hydrogen corridors emerging across Europe and North Africa supported by EU-backed consortia (WHO, 2022) (Hydrogen Europe, 2023). Whether it’s infrastructure, energy, or digital finance, the next decade will reward those who can convene coalitions across borders, sectors, and ideologies.

The Opportunity

This is not a moment to retreat or overcorrect. It’s a moment to lead differently. Governments and enterprises that act now—cooperatively, regionally, and strategically—will be the architects of the next generation of global systems.
