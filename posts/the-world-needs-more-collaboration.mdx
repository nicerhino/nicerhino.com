For decades, the United States played the pivotal role in orchestrating global governance. From the post-war Bretton Woods institutions to the founding of the United Nations and NATO, US leadership underwrote a system of liberal internationalism built on free trade, diplomacy, and multilateral cooperation.

That era is ending. And it’s not because other powers have overtaken the US economically or militarily. It’s because the US is voluntarily stepping back.

From the Trump administration’s withdrawal from the Paris Agreement and the World Health Organization to the Biden administration’s uneven follow-through on global commitments, the US is increasingly seen as a less reliable steward of global leadership. According to the Pew Research Center, global confidence in the US president dropped to 17% in 2020 and rebounded to 75% under Biden, but trust in the US system remains volatile (Pew, 2021). Congressional gridlock, isolationist currents, and a media ecosystem that rewards populism over pragmatism have all eroded the foundations of sustained international engagement.

This retrenchment leaves a vacuum. But it also creates opportunity.

## A More Collaborative Multipolar World

When the dominant actor in a unipolar system disengages, others move in to fill the space. We're already seeing this shift. The EU is pushing forward with its Green Deal, aiming to reduce net greenhouse gas emissions by at least 55% by 2030 (European Commission, 2020), and implementing the Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism to enforce climate-aligned trade rules.

ASEAN, representing over 660 million people and a combined GDP of $3.6 trillion (2022), is strengthening its regional frameworks and signed the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP) in 2020, creating the largest free-trade bloc in the world (World Bank, 2023). The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA), launched in 2021, represents a market of 1.4 billion people and a combined GDP of $3.4 trillion, aiming to increase intra-African trade by 52% by 2025 (Brookings Institution, 2022).

This isn’t just geopolitics. It's economics and values.

In a world of transnational threats—climate change, pandemics, cyberattacks, migration—no single nation can act alone. We need platforms that support cooperation and collective action. The European Union, with all its flaws, remains the most successful modern model of supranational governance. EU GDP stands at over $16 trillion, making it the world’s third-largest economy. Its expansion—geographically, economically, and ideologically—offers a vision of the future that is rules-based, inclusive, and sustainable.

## The Case for EU-Style Regionalism

The EU model isn’t about erasing national identity. It’s about pooling sovereignty to tackle challenges no single country can handle alone. Enlargement in the Balkans, deeper integration with Ukraine, and even enhanced cooperation with North Africa could extend the EU’s stabilising influence. According to the European Parliament, enlargement has historically contributed to peace, democracy, and prosperity in former Eastern Bloc states (European Parliament, 2022).

Other regions can take notes. South America, long fragmented by political differences, could revive discussions around a more functional Mercosur. ASEAN can deepen economic ties and standardize regulations. Even North America—with the right leadership—could evolve NAFTA’s successor, USMCA, into something more forward-looking and equitable.

## Historical Precedent: Don’t Wait for Crisis

The institutions that shape our world today were built in the rubble of World War II. We shouldn’t have to wait for another crisis to build better systems. The decline of US dominance doesn’t have to mean chaos; it can be a catalyst for a more balanced, resilient global order.

We know that trade, done right, reduces poverty. According to the World Bank, trade liberalisation lifted over one billion people out of extreme poverty between 1990 and 2015 (World Bank, 2020). We know that democratic norms don’t spread by accident. And we know that societies thrive when they invest in their neighbours rather than wall them off.

Now is the time for regional powers, coalitions, and organisations to step up—not to mimic the US, but to do better. To cooperate more. To compete less destructively. To build shared prosperity rather than protect zero-sum gains.

## What This Means for Enterprise and Government

For both enterprise and government agencies, this shift isn’t academic. It affects everything from supply chain resilience and regulatory planning to geopolitical risk assessments. The future will be shaped less by bilateral deals and more by regional frameworks, trade blocs, and standard-setting alliances.
