With the United States gradually stepping back from its long-held position as the cornerstone of global governance, a once unthinkable question now drives the international conversation: Who will fill the void?

The U.S.'s strategic withdrawal—from global institutions, trade leadership, and even military commitments—has not occurred in a vacuum. As explored in our previous article, this shift has been both deliberate and destabilising, accelerated by the Trump administration’s isolationist policies and sustained by deeper structural trends in U.S. politics ([Zakaria, 2020]()). Yet amidst the uncertainty lies a generational opportunity for other nations, regions, and multilateral organisations to assume new forms of leadership.

This article explores where those opportunities lie, who is best positioned to seize them, and what kind of leadership the world needs now.

## 1. The European Union: A Model for Cooperative Sovereignty

The European Union remains the most obvious candidate to assume a larger global leadership role. With a population of 447 million and a GDP of over $17 trillion ([World
Bank, 2023](https://data.worldbank.org/)), the EU is already a global economic powerhouse. More importantly, it has built the world’s most advanced model of peaceful multilateral governance.

From climate policy to digital regulation, the EU leads by example. The European Green Deal, Digital Services Act, and GDPR demonstrate how values-based regulation can have global impact ([European Commission, 2023](https://ec.europa.eu/info/strategy/priorities-2019-2024/european-green-deal_en)). The EU’s leadership in climate diplomacy, particularly through its Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), is pushing global supply chains toward decarbonisation ([IEA, 2023](https://www.iea.org/reports/net-zero-by-2050)). It also continues to advance its strategic autonomy agenda, strengthening its capacity to act independently in foreign policy and security ([EEAS, 2023](https://www.eeas.europa.eu/eeas/why-european-strategic-autonomy-matters_en)).

## 2. The African Union and AfCFTA: Building Continental Strength

Africa has long been seen as a continent of potential, but with the African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) now in motion, it is beginning to translate that potential into coordinated power.

AfCFTA, the largest free trade area in the world by number of countries, connects 54 nations and 1.4 billion people. It is expected to boost intra-African trade by over 50% and could lift 30 million people out of extreme poverty, according to the World Bank ([World Bank, 2020](https://data.worldbank.org/)). Combined with growing cooperation through the African Union, the region is poised to become a more cohesive voice on global issues like debt restructuring, climate justice, and vaccine equity.

## 3. ASEAN and the Indo-Pacific: Quiet Integration, Growing Influence

In Southeast Asia, ASEAN has quietly become one of the world’s most stable regional blocs. Through mechanisms like the Regional Comprehensive Economic Partnership (RCEP), which includes China, Japan, South Korea, and Australia, ASEAN is facilitating trade and coordination across an economically dynamic region ([Brookings Institution, 2023](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/rcep-a-new-trade-agreement-that-will-shape-global-economics-and-politics/)).

While ASEAN avoids political entanglements, its pragmatic model of consensus-driven engagement has proven resilient. The Indo-Pacific as a whole is becoming the center of global economic gravity, and its institutions will increasingly shape the rules of trade, investment, and security ([CSIS, 2022](https://www.csis.org/analysis/filling-indo-pacific-economic-framework)).

## 4. Multilateral Institutions: Reinvention or Irrelevance

With U.S. disengagement, the Bretton Woods institutions—the IMF, World Bank, and WTO—face a crossroads. Will they reform to reflect a more multipolar reality, or continue to lose credibility?

Efforts are already underway. The WTO is exploring alternative dispute resolution mechanisms, and the IMF is reconsidering voting shares ([Atlantic Council, 2024](https://www.atlanticcouncil.org/blogs/econographics/understanding-the-debate-over-imf-quota-reform/)). Countries like India, Brazil, and South Africa are demanding a bigger seat at the table, and they’re right to do so. Reforming these bodies to reflect 21st-century power realities is not only just—it’s necessary for their survival.

## 5. Corporate and Civil Society Leadership

Beyond nation-states, global corporations and transnational civil society organisations are increasingly filling governance gaps. Tech companies now wield outsized influence on digital rights and misinformation ([UNESCO, 2023](https://www.unesco.org/en/digital-governance)). Philanthropic organisations like the Gates Foundation have reshaped global health funding ([Lancet, 2020](https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140-6736%2809%2960885-0/fulltext)). And movements for racial justice, climate action, and labor rights are driving cross-border activism.

The private and nonprofit sectors cannot replace states, but they are increasingly shaping global norms. In many cases, they are more agile and forward-looking than traditional institutions.

## What Kind of Leadership Does the World Need?

The emerging vacuum is not just about who leads—it’s about how they lead. The world does not need a new hegemon. It needs coalitions of the willing, built on cooperation, legitimacy, and mutual benefit.

- **Inclusive leadership** that amplifies the voices of historically marginalised countries.
- **Sustainable leadership** that prioritises climate action, social equity, and economic resilience.
- **Collaborative leadership** that rejects zero-sum thinking in favor of shared outcomes.

## Conclusion: A New Chapter for Global Governance

The age of American primacy may be ending, but that does not mean global leadership must be rudderless. In fact, this transition opens space for a richer, more representative, and more humane international order.
